Selected projects, in reverse chronological order

Sep 2022 – Nov 2025

Flexible ventral stimulator for spinal cord injury repair

My most recent project was an implant designed to treat spinal cord injury. Injury leaves a fluid-filled lesion at the site, severing neurons that once spanned the region. Those damaged neurons rarely recover on their own, and electrical stimulation is one way to encourage regeneration — but it only works if it's delivered exactly where it's needed. In rats and humans alike, the ventral side of the spinal cord carries the circuitry for lower-limb movement, so stimulating that region can produce real restorative effects. It's also one of the hardest places to reach, requiring a delicate surgery and an equally delicate device.

Sep 2022 – Nov 2025

Light-sensitive hydrogel for spinal cord injury repair

Alongside the stimulator work, I helped develop an injectable hydrogel for spinal cord injury. Injured neurons aren't just lethargic — they're picky about what they grow through, and a fluid-filled lesion offers no structure at all. The proposed fix is to inject a hydrogel that solidifies inside the lesion, giving neurons a scaffold to regenerate along. Our lab chose a blue-light-sensitive gel specifically because it allows finer control over solidification during surgery, unlike typical enzymatic gels that start setting the moment they're mixed — putting real time pressure on the surgeon.

My job was to find the best way to expose the gel for uniform solidification throughout the injury site. I measured the gel's optical properties, built Monte Carlo simulation software to model different exposure setups, and verified the results against the surgical procedure used in rat studies.

Sep 2020 – Aug 2022

Microbial fuel cell payload for experimental rocketry competitions

I led a student team building a payload for two very different competitions: the Canadian Reduced Gravity Experiment Design Challenge (CAN RGX), flown on a parabolic flight that simulates microgravity, and the International Rocketry Engineering Competition (IREC), where a sounding rocket delivers a 3-unit CubeSat payload. Each imposed its own design constraints, so we ended up building two distinct versions of the same idea.

That idea was a microbial fuel cell — a bacteria-powered battery. It sounds unrelated to space travel, but there are real applications: wastewater treatment and methane production, where methane can double as rocket propellant. Doing useful work while generating power is hard to argue with.

As lead, I handled the administrative and mentoring side, but I was also the systems integrator and, as the only biologist on the team, the biological lead. Electrical, mechanical, and biological design all had to work together — my first real introduction to systems engineering, and probably why I enjoyed the systems work on the stimulator so much later on. This project also ran through the pandemic; despite the toll that took, I managed to keep the team together.

Sep 2021 – May 2022

Opioid overdose detection and reversal device

As my capstone project, I led a team designing a device to detect and reverse opioid overdose. I designed and built the detection half: a chest strap that tracks breathing rate, since overdose kills by stopping respiration — so measuring breath directly seemed like the most direct signal to act on.

The strap itself is spandex fitted with a strain gauge. As the chest expands with each breath, the spandex stretches and the gauge picks up the tension. Once filtered — electronically through the circuitry, then digitally on the Arduino — the result is a clean readout of a person's breathing, with rate found from the peak-to-peak timing of the signal.

A device like this only works if it fits everyone, so I tested it at a capstone conference at UBC, inviting students, professors, and guests to strap it on and just breathe. It worked on every body type in the room, including through thick winter jackets — the strap buckles on in seconds, like a seatbelt, which matters just as much as the sensing itself if anyone's actually going to wear it.
